1. The Hardware "Cheat Code": Get an ESP32-CAM-MB
Instead of buying a raw FTDI FT232RL programmer and dealing with loose jumper wires, buy an ESP32-CAM-MB programmer shield (usually around ₱100-₱150).
Why it saves time: The ESP32-CAM-MB snaps directly onto the bottom of the ESP32-CAM. You just plug a micro-USB cable directly into the board and hit "Upload." No wiring TX/RX pins, no manually grounding GPIO0, and no holding down reset buttons to flash your code. It turns a 20-minute frustrating wiring process into a 5-second plug-and-play process.
2. The Power Shortcut to Prevent Brownouts
The number one reason ESP32-CAM cars fail during testing is power drops. When the L298N motor driver turns the wheels, it pulls current, causing the ESP32-CAM's voltage to drop, which immediately crashes the camera stream or reboots the web server.
The fix: Wire your two 18650 batteries directly into the L298N's 12V input. Then, use the 5V output pin on the L298N to power the ESP32-CAM. Do not try to power the motors and the ESP32 from the exact same parallel node without utilizing the L298N's onboard voltage regulator.
3. The Software "Cheat Code": Don't Write the Streamer from Scratch
Writing an MJPEG video streaming server in C++ from scratch will take weeks.
The fix: Open the Arduino IDE, go to File > Examples > ESP32 > Camera > CameraWebServer.
This built-in sketch already contains the highly complex camera initialization and web streaming protocols. You just need to add the code for the L298N motor driver (defining your GPIO pins for forward, backward, left, right) and inject HTML buttons into the existing web interface to trigger those motor functions.

MakerLab doesn't publish a dedicated assembly page for this kit, and the official guide document requires a Scribd subscription. But this chassis is a standard design used by hundreds of similar kits worldwide, and I know exactly how it goes together. Here's your complete step-by-step guide.

Step 1 — Inventory Check
Lay everything out on a flat surface and verify you have all parts before starting:

1× acrylic base plate
4× TT DC geared motors
4× metal motor brackets
4× rubber wheels
1× L298N motor driver module
1× ESP32-CAM board
2× ESP32-CAM support frames
1× 18650 battery holder
Screw sets (M3×10, M3×30, M2×10, M2×25, M3×8 flat head)
Copper pillars (M3×18 double pass)
Nickel-plated nuts (M3 and M2)
Pre-wired XH2.54 connectors
Screwdriver (included)
Step 2 — Attach Motors to Brackets
Each TT motor slots into a metal L-shaped bracket. The motor body sits inside the bracket opening and is secured with 2× M2×10 screws and M2 nuts through the motor's mounting holes. Do all four motors. Keep motor wire leads pointing inward toward the chassis center — this keeps wiring tidy later.

⚠️ Critical: Note which way the motor shaft points before securing. All four shafts should point outward (away from the chassis center) to accept the wheels.

Step 3 — Mount Motor Brackets to Acrylic Base
The acrylic base has four corner mounting positions for the brackets. Use M3×10 countersunk screws with M3 nuts to bolt each bracket to the base. The flat head of the countersunk screw sits flush with the bottom of the acrylic so the car sits level.

⚠️ Acrylic warning: Do not overtighten. Acrylic cracks under excessive torque. Snug is enough — if you feel resistance, stop.

Orientation: two motors on the left side, two on the right. Front and back motors on each side should spin the same direction when driven.

Step 4 — Mount Copper Pillars for Upper Components
The M3×18 double-pass copper pillars screw into the acrylic base at the designated mounting holes near the center-rear area. These create standoffs that will elevate the L298N and battery holder above the base plate. Thread them in finger-tight, then a quarter turn with the screwdriver.

Step 5 — Mount the Battery Holder
The 18650 battery holder mounts on the underside or lower layer of the chassis using M3 screws through the holder's mounting tabs. Orient it so the power switch (if it has one) and wire leads face toward the rear of the car for easy access. Insert your two Panasonic NCR18650B cells now — pay attention to polarity markings (+ and −) on the holder.

Step 6 — Mount the L298N Motor Driver
The L298N sits on top of the copper pillar standoffs. Use M3×10 screws through the L298N's corner mounting holes down into the pillar tops. The L298N's screw terminal block (where motors connect) should face toward the front of the car, and the power input terminals should face toward the battery.

Step 7 — Connect Motors to L298N
This is where the pre-wired XH2.54 connectors from the kit come in.

Wire the motors to the L298N output terminals as follows:

L298N Terminal	Motors Connected
OUT1 and OUT2	Left side motors (both wired in parallel)
OUT3 and OUT4	Right side motors (both wired in parallel)
For each side, the two motor wire pairs (red and black) connect in parallel — meaning both red wires share one terminal and both black wires share the other. The kit's 6-pin XH2.54 connector handles this grouping.

⚠️ Don't worry about polarity yet. If wheels spin backwards when you test, you simply swap the two wires on that side at the terminal block. You'll verify this during firmware testing.

Step 8 — Power Wiring to L298N
Connect the battery holder's output wires to the L298N's power input terminal block:

Red wire (positive) → 12V terminal on L298N
Black wire (negative) → GND terminal on L298N
Leave the 5V enable jumper on the L298N in place (the small jumper cap on the 5V pin). This allows the L298N's onboard regulator to output 5V which will power your ESP32-CAM.

Step 9 — Mount ESP32-CAM Support Frames
The two acrylic support frames hold the ESP32-CAM upright at the front of the chassis. Attach the frames to the base using M2×25 self-tapping screws. The frames have a slot or clip where the ESP32-CAM board slides in. Mount this assembly at the front-center of the upper chassis level so the camera faces forward.

Step 10 — Attach Wheels
Push each rubber wheel onto the motor shaft. The shaft has a D-shaped flat side — align the wheel hub's flat side to match before pushing. Press firmly but do not force. If a wheel feels very stiff, check alignment before applying more pressure.

Step 11 — Final Wiring Between ESP32-CAM and L298N
Using your M-F jumper wires, connect the ESP32-CAM GPIO pins to the L298N control inputs:

ESP32-CAM GPIO	L298N Pin	Function
GPIO14	IN1	Left motor direction A
GPIO15	IN2	Left motor direction B
GPIO2	IN3	Right motor direction A
GPIO4	IN4	Right motor direction B
GND	GND	Common ground
For power to ESP32-CAM:

L298N 5V output pin → ESP32-CAM 5V pin
L298N GND → ESP32-CAM GND
For the HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor (mount it at the front using your bracket):

HC-SR04 VCC → 5V
HC-SR04 GND → GND
HC-SR04 TRIG → GPIO13
HC-SR04 ECHO → GPIO12 (note: verify this works at boot — if the car fails to start, remap to GPIO13/15)
Step 12 — Double-Check Before Powering On
Before inserting batteries or flipping the power switch, go through this checklist:

All four wheels spin freely by hand with no obstruction
No bare wire ends are touching each other or the acrylic
Battery holder polarity is correct (red to 12V, black to GND on L298N)
The 5V jumper on L298N is in place
ESP32-CAM is seated properly in its support frame
Camera ribbon cable is fully inserted and the connector latch is closed
Once all boxes are checked, you're ready to move to firmware flashing with your undercarrier board.

static ra_filter_t * ra_filter_init(ra_filter_t * filter, size_t sample_size){
    memset(filter, 0, sizeof(ra_filter_t));
    filter->values = (int *)malloc(sample_size * sizeof(int));
    if(!filter->values){
        return NULL;
    }
    memset(filter->values, 0, sample_size * sizeof(int));
    filter->size = sample_size;
    return filter;
}
static int ra_filter_run(ra_filter_t * filter, int value){
    if(!filter->values){
        return value;
    }
    filter->sum -= filter->values[filter->index];
    filter->values[filter->index] = value;
    filter->sum += filter->values[filter->index];
    filter->index++;
    filter->index = filter->index % filter->size;
    if (filter->count < filter->size) {
        filter->count++;
    }
    return filter->sum / filter->count;
}
static size_t jpg_encode_stream(void * arg, size_t index, const void* data, size_t len){
    jpg_chunking_t *j = (jpg_chunking_t *)arg;
    if(!index){
        j->len = 0;
    }
    if(httpd_resp_send_chunk(j->req, (const char *)data, len) != ESP_OK){
        return 0;
    }
    j->len += len;
    return len;
}

            _jpg_buf = NULL;
        } else if(_jpg_buf){
            free(_jpg_buf);
            _jpg_buf = NULL;
        }
        if(res != ESP_OK){
            break;
        }
        int64_t fr_end = esp_timer_get_time();
        int64_t frame_time = fr_end - last_frame;
        last_frame = fr_end;
        frame_time /= 1000;
        uint32_t avg_frame_time = ra_filter_run(&ra_filter, frame_time);
        Serial.printf("MJPG: %uB %ums (%.1ffps), AVG: %ums (%.1ffps)"
            ,(uint32_t)(_jpg_buf_len),
            (uint32_t)frame_time, 1000.0 / (uint32_t)frame_time,
            avg_frame_time, 1000.0 / avg_frame_time
        );
    }
    last_frame = 0;
    return res;
}

Claude responded: On increasing WiFi signal strength via software:
On increasing WiFi signal strength via software:

The ESP32's WiFi transmit power can be increased in code. Add this line in ESP32CAM_Car.ino inside the setup() function, right after WiFi.begin(ssid, password):

cpp
WiFi.setTxPower(WIFI_POWER_19_5dBm);
This sets the transmitter to maximum power. The default is around 14dBm so this is a meaningful boost. However the practical gain will be modest since you already have an external antenna, which contributes more to range than software power settings. Do not expect dramatic improvement -- maybe 10-20% more range.

On Bluetooth as an alternative or supplement:

This is worth considering seriously. The idea would be to use Bluetooth for motor control commands while keeping WiFi only for the camera stream. The benefit is that Bluetooth and WiFi share the same antenna on the ESP32, but splitting the workload means WiFi is not competing with control packets for bandwidth -- the stream gets the full WiFi pipe, and motor commands travel over Bluetooth which has lower latency for short commands.

The practical implementation would use the ESP32's built-in Bluetooth Serial (BT SPP) for directional commands from a phone app like Serial Bluetooth Terminal, while the camera stream stays on the browser via WiFi. This is a moderately complex firmware change but very feasible for a third-year CS group.

However given your deadline is April 30 and the car is already fully functional, I would not recommend attempting this now. The risk of introducing bugs close to the deadline outweighs the benefit. The WiFi transmit power increase is the only change worth making right now -- it is a one-line addition with no risk.
